CASE STUDY · JETSTAR

Baggage

Turning a page shaped by separate requests into one clear cohesive experience, without giving up revenue.

Role
Senior product designer
Platform
Desktop & mobile
Company
Jetstar
Timeline
24 months

The challenge

Baggage is Jetstar’s biggest source of ancillary revenue, so the page never stands still. Each new commercial goal adds something to it. Airlines are also legally required to tell customers about fees and allowances, and over time that turned into a stack of banners.

My job was to balance three things:

  • Revenue: meet the commercial targets
  • Regulation: inform customers properly
  • Clarity: keep it simple enough to choose with confidence

The work arrived as separate projects. Over time, I shaped it into one experience.

01. Initiatives

Carry-on selection rules update

In August 2026, Jetstar changed its carry-on rules. Instead of weighing bags at the gate, the standard fare now includes one small underseat bag, and customers can pay for overhead locker space to bring a second.

My challenge was to turn complex business rules into a simple choice customers could understand, so customers aren’t surprised by a fee at the airport.

It was a high-visibility project with many stakeholders, and the first release wasn’t perfect. As soon as it shipped, I gathered customer feedback and simplified the design.

Before: Carry-on was 7kg vs 14kg.

First release: Two bag options, with descriptions on the right.

Updated: Simplified the options by showing the underseat bag is included and the overhead bag is optional, with an image to show where the bags go.

Individual bags page

The individual bags page was overdue for a reno. Most customers add bags for all passengers at once, and the business worried that making individual selection too easy would impact revenue. This is a feature particularly useful for families travelling with kids, without needing the baggage for every person.

The existing page had many usability issues, mostly because options were hidden behind a stepper. We ran rounds of usability testing on different patterns for desktop and mobile to find the most efficient design.

On desktop

Showing every weight option on the page let customers compare prices at a glance. Many moved up to a heavier bag once they saw how little more it cost, which the old stepper hid. Testing also showed group travellers wanted to double-check their total without doing the maths, so we added a total weight summary.

On mobile

We tested a horizontal scroll for weights, but customers found it harder to use. We built an expandable selector instead, since vertical scrolling felt more intuitive across all ages. Customers can review each passenger’s bags and see the combined weight.

The impact

  • Fewer customers chose 15kg, as seeing the price of every option led them to buy heavier bags.
  • 788 extra bag selections during the trial, mostly 25kg and 40kg, driven by mobile.
  • In an A/B test, baggage revenue rose $0.50 per passenger, forecast at $130k a year at scale.

02. Future state

How bags fits with the entire booking flow

As I started to get to know our customers more and more, after many rounds of user testing, I worked closely with my PM to ideate the next phase of bags page. Or do we really need a page?

Bags are the most important factor when customers choose a bundle. The bounce rate between these pages is high. To make it easier for customers to see the value of bundles and compare their options, bringing these two sections together is crucial to providing a better booking experience.

Reflection

Good design doesn’t always ship in one go. The first carry-on release wasn’t where I wanted it, but by listening to stakeholders’ concerns and bringing customer evidence to every conversation, I moved the team step by step towards a simpler, clearer experience.
